# Product and audience analysis

6 minutes inside the 30-minute research clock in the fast version, 15 to 20 minutes standalone. One question: **who pays, who uses, and where do those two disagree?**

In B2B, the gap between buyer and daily user is where most of the work lives, and it is the thing almost nobody mentions in a cover letter. Naming it signals you have done this before.

## The four cells

One line each. Guess if you must, and mark the guess.

| | Who | What they want | How you know |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Buyer** | Job title who signs | The outcome they report upward | Pricing page, case studies, sales copy |
| **Champion** | Who runs the pilot | A rollout that does not embarrass them | Job ad language, docs, community |
| **Daily user** | Who is in it every day | To finish the task and close the tab | Product UI, support docs, reviews |
| **Blocker** | Who can veto | Compliance, security, or their own workload | Security page, certifications, integrations |

Sometimes the buyer is the user, which is itself the interesting fact. For a consumer product, replace the table with: who decides to try it, who keeps using it, who churns and why.

## The divergence

One sentence:

> The buyer wants ______, the daily user wants ______, and the product currently favours ______.

Real answers sound like:

- "The buyer wants a compliance audit trail, the analyst wants to finish a review in under 10 minutes, and the product favours the audit trail. Every action needs a justification field."
- "The buyer wants adoption dashboards, the end user wants to be left alone, and the product favours the buyer, which is why the in-app prompts are so aggressive."
- "The buyer and the user are the same person, a solo ops lead, so one person does configuration and daily work in the same interface at the same density."

Whichever side the product favours, the neglected side is usually where your challenge is hiding.

## Sources, in order of speed

1. **Pricing page.** Seat-based means individual users matter. Platform-based means procurement matters.
2. **Case studies.** The quoted job title is the buyer. The quoted metric is what the buyer reports upward.
3. **The job ad.** Read it as a document about their problems. "Comfortable with ambiguity" means the roadmap is not settled. "Partner closely with sales" means you get pulled into demos. "Improve activation" means users drop off early.
4. **Support docs and community.** Find the longest article. Length correlates with confusion.
5. **G2 or Capterra.** Read the 3-star reviews only. 5-star is marketing, 1-star is billing disputes, 3-star is usability.

## Jobs to be done, fast version

> When ______, [user] wants to ______, so they can ______.

One job. If you write three, you have written none.

## What to write in the dossier

```markdown
## Product and audience

**Buyer:** [title] wants [outcome]
**Champion:** [title] wants [outcome]
**Daily user:** [title] wants [outcome]
**Blocker:** [title or none]

**The primary job:** When [trigger], [user] wants to [action], so they can [outcome].

**The divergence:** The buyer wants [X], the daily user wants [Y], and the product currently favours [Z].

**Where that shows up in the product:** [specific screen or element]

**Confidence:** high / medium / guessing
```

If you are guessing, the letter hedges visibly, which reads as honest rather than uninformed.

## Skip conditions

- Single-user tool with no procurement layer: note buyer equals user, move on in 2 minutes.
- You cannot see the product and there are no reviews or docs: write "insufficient evidence, letter will speak to their stated problem from the job ad instead." Do not invent an audience. Inventing one is how you end up confidently wrong in a first call.
